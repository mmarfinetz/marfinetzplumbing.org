Sonde Locator

Find a sewer-camera sonde's frequency and depth with an iPhone. You need a small pickup coil plugged into the phone (see What to plug in).

    3 · Locate & depth

    1. Hold the coil level, about a foot off the ground. Sweep across the line. The signal peaks right over the pipe.
    2. At the strongest spot, rotate the coil until the signal peaks. That's the direction of the pipe. Keep the coil pointed that way.
    3. Walk along the pipe. The biggest peak is directly over the sonde. A weaker "ghost" peak (about ⅕ as strong) can appear beyond each end.

    Calibrate once per coil and camera. After that the depth reads live at the top while you stand over the peak.

    1. Lay the camera head on the ground before pushing it in.
    2. Hold the coil level, pointed along the head, at a known height above it.
    3. Hold still and tap Calibrate.

    No calibration needed. Stand on the peak, take a reading at your normal height, lift the coil straight up, and take another. Lift by at least a third of the expected depth for a good result.

      Settings

      What to plug in

      A sonde gives off a magnetic field, not sound, so the iPhone can't detect it on its own. The microphone can't hear it. Safari can't read the phone's compass sensor, and that sensor is far too slow and insensitive anyway: about 100 readings a second against a 512 Hz signal, and roughly a thousand times too weak. A pickup coil turns the field into a small voltage, the iPhone's audio input digitises it, and this page does the rest.

      1 · The coil
      • Quick start: a suction-cup telephone pickup coil, the kind sold for recording phone calls, with a 3.5 mm plug. It's small, so its range is short.
      • Better range: wind your own on a 10 mm × 200 mm ferrite rod (an AM-radio antenna rod). Use 2,000–3,000 turns of 36–38 AWG enamelled magnet wire; a drill on slow speed makes this quick. Twist the two leads together.
      • Mount the coil on a stick, a foot or more away from the phone. Phones are magnetically noisy.
      2 · Getting it into the iPhone
      • Easiest: a USB audio interface with a guitar (instrument) input. It plugs straight into an iPhone 15 or newer. Older iPhones need Apple's Lightning to USB 3 Camera Adapter. Plug the coil into the instrument input and turn the gain up.
      • Cheapest: Apple's USB-C (or Lightning) to 3.5 mm adapter and a 4-pole (TRRS) plug wired as shown. The resistor makes iOS see a microphone. The capacitor keeps the microphone bias voltage out of the coil.
      3 · Tips & troubleshooting
      • The page must be opened over https. iPhone blocks the audio input on plain http pages.
      • In Safari, tap Share → Add to Home Screen to open it like an app. The screen stays awake while it runs.
      • Check the input name under Start. If it says "iPhone Microphone", the adapter wasn't picked up. Unplug it, plug it back in, and tap Start again.
      • Strong 60 Hz hum near a house means the coil is working. Hum fading outdoors, away from wiring, is normal.
      • "Input clipping" means the signal is too strong. Turn the interface gain down or move the coil away from power cables.
      • Recalibrate if you change the coil, adapter, interface gain or camera.
      • Rough expectation, not field-tested: a few feet with a telephone pickup, more with a wound ferrite coil and an interface with adjustable gain. A dedicated locator will still reach deeper.

      Depth readings assume a level sonde and a coil held directly over it. A tilted sonde, nearby metal, other utilities and nearby power cables all skew the result. Confirm before digging, and call 811 before you dig.
